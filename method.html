Shared procedure

How we measured PLAY

Every environment was tested the same way: one participant at a time, a tutorial environment first, then the affective environment, with questionnaires at three points and a fixed rule for what counts as success.

Study design

Each environment was evaluated in a within-subjects user study with 25 participants, meaning every participant experienced both the tutorial environment and the affective environment, and was compared against themselves. Sessions ran one participant at a time and lasted about 50 minutes.

Why a tutorial environment

First-time VR users feel pleasure and excitement simply from using VR, and they also get frustrated learning the controls. Both would show up in the questionnaires and neither is caused by the affective environment. So before the affective environment, every participant completed a tutorial environment: a visually plain scene with tasks for teleporting, snap-turning, grabbing objects and throwing objects. The participant's state after the tutorial is treated as their neutral baseline, because any novelty or frustration present there would be present in the affective environment too.

Three readings

Questionnaires were completed at three points, which the papers call P1, P2 and P3.

The seven parts of PLAY

PLAY is defined as the highly pleasurable, fast-paced series of back-and-forth interactions between two or more beings, generalised to social joy. We broke that definition into measurable parts so that elicitation could be checked from several directions at once. The raw feeling of PLAY is joy with high valence (pleasantness), high arousal (activation) and high dominance (control). What makes it PLAY rather than any other pleasant feeling is that the interaction is social and reciprocal. The ANSS adds a direct reading of the PLAY system itself.

Participants and apparatus

Participants were recruited through course WhatsApp groups and direct messages at UCT. There were no inclusion criteria, but we excluded anyone under 18, anyone with an existing psychiatric disorder or on chronic stimulant medication, anyone scoring 20 or above on the Beck Depression Inventory, and anyone with a history of motion sickness. Psychiatric disorders and depression change emotional reactivity and reduce social play, and motion sickness predicts cybersickness, all of which would distort the readings. Ethics clearance was granted by the UCT Inter-Faculty Research Ethics Committee, reference IFHREC/02979/2026.

Sessions ran in the dedicated VR room in the UCT Computer Science building on a 2018 HTC Vive Pro headset with two controllers, driven by a Windows 10 desktop with a six-core Intel i5, 16 GB of RAM and an NVIDIA GTX 1070. The environments were built in Unity 6.

The rule for success

Each instrument has one or more hypotheses attached to it. Together they say what the environment had to do for us to claim it elicited PLAY.

Analysis rules

  • Every test is two-sided at the 5% level, because an environment could move a reading in the wrong direction.
  • Each dataset is checked for normality with the Shapiro-Wilk test and a Q-Q plot. If it passes, the parametric test is used (t-test); if not, the non-parametric equivalent is used (Wilcoxon signed-rank).
  • Where several tests are run as a group, p-values are Holm-Bonferroni corrected.
  • Reciprocity is tested by comparing the proportion of NPC throws the participant answered against 1000 time-shifted surrogate copies of their own throw record, which keep their throwing rhythm but break any alignment with the NPC. If the observed proportion is no higher than the surrogate average, the participant was throwing on their own rhythm.
  • PLAY is claimed only if every stipulated hypothesis is significant. A partial result, however strong, is reported as parts met rather than as PLAY elicited.
